Combining Independent Smart Beta Strategies
for Portfolio Optimization
Phil Maguire, Karl Moffett, and Rebecca Maguire
Department of Computer Science, National University of Ireland Maynooth, Ireland
Department of Psychology, National University of Ireland Maynooth, Ireland

Abstract. Smart beta, also known as strategic beta or factor investing, is the idea of
selecting an investment portfolio in a simple rule-based manner that systematically
captures market inefficiencies, thereby enhancing risk-adjusted returns above
capitalization-weighted benchmarks. We explore the idea of applying a smart strategy in
reverse, yielding a "bad beta" portfolio which can be shorted, thus allowing long and short
positions on independent smart beta strategies to generate beta neutral returns. In this
article we detail the construction of a monthly reweighted portfolio involving two
independent smart beta strategies; the first component is a long-short beta-neutral
strategy derived from running an adaptive boosting classifier on a suite of momentum
indicators. The second component is a minimized volatility portfolio which exploits the
observation that low-volatility stocks tend to yield higher risk-adjusted returns than
high-volatility stocks. Working off a market benchmark Sharpe Ratio of 0.42, we find that
the market neutral component achieves a ratio of 0.61, the low volatility approach achieves
a ratio of 0.90, while the combined leveraged strategy achieves a ratio of 0.96. In six
months of live trading, the combined strategy achieved a Sharpe Ratio of 1.35. These
results reinforce the effectiveness of smart beta strategies, and demonstrate that
combining multiple strategies simultaneously can yield better performance than that
achieved by any single component in isolation.

Keywords: smart beta; factor investing; low-volatility anomaly; portfolio optimization;
variance minimization; minimum volatility portfolio; long-short strategy, diversification

1 Introduction
According to the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), stock returns should be a linear
function of beta. [E(Ri) = Rf + βi(E(Rm) − Rf) + α]

===== PAGE 2 =====
where Rf is the risk-free rate of return, βi is a measure of the correlation between
portfolio i's returns and that of a benchmark, and Rm is the expected returns of that
benchmark asset (Perold, 2004). The elusive α term is the excess returns that are
generated from selecting long positions which outperform the market, or short positions
that underperform. In an efficient market (see Malkiel & Fama, 1970), the expected value
of the alpha coefficient is zero.

Recent work on smart beta has suggested that superior risk-to-reward performance can be
achieved by following a few simple rules. Low-volatility portfolios across the world have
offered a desirable combination of high average returns and small drawdowns, "the
greatest anomaly in finance" (Baker et al., 2011).

Assets under management in smart-beta strategies ballooned from $103bn in 2008 to
$616bn in 2015. Investors continued to pile into smart-beta funds in Q1 2017, driving a
2,000% rise in new money allocated to such strategies. Smart beta funds now account for
one seventh of the $4 trillion invested in the global ETF market.

There remains some debate as to whether smart beta is real, insofar as it can be reliably
and consistently exploited in practice. Many factors which appear supportive of smart beta
may be nothing more than temporary anomalies discovered through data mining (Arnott,
Beck, Kalesnik & West, 2016). These anomalies may be small enough to be eroded by
transaction costs, or reflect a statistical quirk such as future bias. Arnott et al. (2016)
suggest smart beta strategies are not sustainable -- soaring popularity pushes prices up in
a way that inflates past performance ("smart beta bubble").

===== PAGE 3 =====
Our study seeks specifically to address whether applying a combination of smart beta
strategies together can deliver real profits beyond the market benchmark. We apply a
monthly reweighting strategy (minimal trading costs, little rebalancing required). We apply
our strategy out-of-sample, developing the portfolio on historical data and applying it to
real live data, "thus removing any possibility of future bias." We investigate whether
combining multiple independent strategies enhances performance.

2 Quantopian
Algorithms tested via the online crowd-sourced hedge fund Quantopian: Python-based
research environment, minute-by-minute US stock pricing data, fundamental business
data, backtesting suite with commission/slippage/bid-ask spread cost models. As of July
2016, ~85,000 members, $250 million under direction.

2.1 Characteristics of a Lucrative Hedge-Fund
Quantopian grants capital allocations to top algorithms meeting strict requirements:

Low Exposure to the Market: β = Covariance(Ri,Rm) / Variance(Rm). Algorithms should
have a beta coefficient between -0.3 and +0.3 vs S&P 500, achieved via hedging.

===== PAGE 4 =====
Consistent Profitability: Portfolios should consistently demonstrate a Sharpe Ratio
greater than 1 (Sharpe = (portfolio return − risk-free rate) / std dev of return series).

Actively Trading Algorithms: Portfolios must rebalance at least once per month or at most
twice a day (ensures performance reflects algorithm quality, not lucky stock selection).

Low Correlation to Peers: Average pairwise correlation of positions to other users'
algorithms must be between -30% and +30%.

3 Beta-neutral portfolio
A beta-neutral strategy remains "market neutral," equity evenly divided between long and
short positions (Sorensen, Hua & Qian, 2007: market neutrality of long-short equity
portfolios leads to higher risk-adjusted return). With beta ~0, the CAPM beta term is
eliminated, leaving alpha as the only return driver ("pure alpha").

Granizo-Mackenzie (2016): take a universe of stocks (e.g. S&P 500 constituents), rank by a
predetermined factor, go long top N, short bottom N. Returns generated from divergence
of the two groups. N must be large enough to limit idiosyncratic risk; too-large N pushes
cumulative beta toward 1 (this is the

===== PAGE 5 =====
smart beta portfolio; the short side, "bad-beta portfolio," has beta ~-1 / "alternative beta").
Combining the two should yield overall beta ~0.

This paper's idea: use momentum oscillators for the Granizo-MacKenzie ranking. Four
indicators used: Relative Strength Index, Stochastic Oscillator, Moving Average Crossover,
and (Dollar) Volume.

Relative Strength Index (RSI): RSI = 100 − 100/(1+RS), RS = (avg x-day gains)/(avg x-day
losses). Calculated over a 14-day window.

Stochastic Oscillator: two signals %k and %D (%D = 3-period moving average of %k, to
smooth it).
%k = (Latest Close − Lowest price of last N days) / (Highest price of last N days − Lowest
price of last N days)

===== PAGE 6 =====
N = 21 days (average trading days in a month) was used to calculate %k, mirroring the
return-analysis period. Only the %D signal (smoothed) was used, as less noisy / better
suited to the machine-learning process.

Moving Average Crossover: related to MACD. The paper analyses the latest MACD value
directly (not charted). Granizo-Mackenzie warns that choosing specific chart values can
cause overfitting; kept general by using:
MAcrossover = (1-month simple moving average) / (2-month simple moving average)

Volume: Quantopian's "average dollar volume" factor (average traded volume over a
lookback window x latest share price) used instead of raw volume, as a liquidity measure.
Remorov (2014): trading volume relates to price during market-crash downturns.

Normalisation: simplest method used -- rank each feature relative to all other stocks (1 =
highest value, 500 = lowest, i.e. cross-sectional rank across the ~500-stock universe).

3.1 Model Selection
Used scikit-learn; instantiated a range of classifiers, cross-validated to assess predictive
robustness.

===== PAGE 7 =====
Same train/test sets used per classifier, across multiple different periods (repeated cross
validation). AdaBoostClassifier was the most effective (Adaptive Boosting meta-algorithm,
Freund & Schapire 1999; less prone to overfitting than some alternatives).

Cross-validation accuracy of 53%. "The margin of success was fine, but if used wisely,
could prove significant." Training window: previous TWO MONTHS was found optimal.
Longer periods: classifier too slow to adapt / spans multiple regimes, ineffective learning.
Shorter (1 month): not enough samples to learn adequately.

3.2 Fundamental Factors
Stone, Chen & White (2014): 8 factor categories (valuation, growth, quality, efficiency,
momentum, risk/size, pay-out, profitability), 50+ financial factors. Quantopian supplies
Morningstar fundamental data. Authors added all implementable factors to training sets,
retrained, inspected feature importance: the original momentum factors were the top 3
features (most consistent predictors). Average Dollar Volume scored lower -- attributed to
the test period being an uptrend (volume matters more in downtrends, per Remorov 2014).

A version including fundamental factors alongside momentum/volume factors did NOT
enhance performance ("inconsistent results"); fundamental factors were dropped entirely.
Cites Jawade, Naidu & Agrawal (2015): fundamental factors can't anticipate price
movement since they've already influenced current price.

3.3 The Effects of Unstable Trading Periods
Algorithm performed poorly immediately AFTER extremely unstable trading periods (though
performance DURING such periods wasn't notably different) -- inferred as poor learning
from highly volatile training data.

===== PAGE 8 =====
Examined S&P 500 monthly high/low/close, 1997-2007, log-normalized monthly ranges
(max−min)/average. [Figure 1: density plots of normalised and logged monthly ranges,
dotted line marks the cutoff.]

Found: omitting the most unstable trading periods (range > 17%, per Figure 1's dotted line)
from the LEARNING/TRAINING process increased performance. Rationale: such high-vol
periods are transient / not representative of the current trend.

3.4 Performance of Momentum Oscillator Quintiles
Universe: 500 S&P 500 constituents. Formed 5 equal-weighted long-only quintile
portfolios by the ML classifier's ranking, each rebalanced monthly. [Figure 2: cumulative
performance of Quintiles 1-5, 2007-2016; visibly monotonic separation, Quintile 5 (top
ranked) highest, Quintile 1 (bottom ranked) lowest.] Interpreted as evidence the
momentum-oscillator ranking is a valid stock-ranking system for a long-short strategy.

===== PAGE 9 =====
[Figure 2 continued/shown: five quintile equity curves, Jan 2007 - 2016, in the range
0-2,500,000 starting near 1,000,000-ish; Quintile 5 (green) tops out highest, ~2.4M by
2016; Quintile 1 (red) lowest, ~1.4M.]

4 Minimum Variance Portfolio
Low-volatility investing has strong empirical support. Ang et al. (2009): across 23
international developed markets, stocks with high recent idiosyncratic volatility had lower
future average returns (adjusted difference −1.31%/month between highest and lowest
volatility quintiles), individually significant in every G7 country -- a global phenomenon,
not sample-specific.

Blitz & van Vliet (2007): global low-vs-high-volatility decile portfolios' annual alpha spread
= 12% over 1986-2006, independent effects in US/European/Japanese markets.

===== PAGE 10 =====
Baker & Haugen (2012): 33 markets, 1990-2011 (incl. non-survivors), volatility of total
return over trailing 24 months, ranked into deciles. In all 21 developed countries, lowest-
volatility decile had both lower risk AND higher return -> large Sharpe divergence.

Clarke, de Silva & Thorley (2006): min-variance portfolios (1,000 largest US stocks,
1968-2005) achieved ~25% volatility reduction with comparable or higher average returns
than the market.

Beta relates to volatility (market beta=1 by definition). Black (1993): 1931-1965,
low-beta US stocks outperformed CAPM predictions, high-beta stocks underperformed.

Frazzini & Pedersen (2014): high-beta portfolios have LOWER alphas/Sharpe than
low-beta portfolios (US equities, 20 intl equity markets, treasury/corporate bonds, futures).
Betting-against-beta (BAB) factor (long leveraged low-beta, short high-beta) produces
significant positive risk-adjusted returns rivaling value/momentum/size in magnitude,
significance, robustness. Hypothesis: constrained investors "stretch for return" by
increasing beta, inflating high-beta security prices; mutual funds/individual investors hold
betas significantly >1; leveraged buyout funds and Berkshire Hathaway (who have leverage
access) buy betas <1 and apply leverage -- "Warren Buffett gets rich by betting against
beta."

Motivated by this evidence: authors employ a second smart-beta strategy, minimizing
volatility.

4.1 Minimum Variance Portfolio Algorithm
Mostowfi & Stier (2013): MVPs are popular for their straightforward covariance-matrix-
based calculation. Based on Frazzini & Pedersen (2014), authors pre-select LOW-BETA
stocks for the MVP. Beta computed vs S&P 500 over a

===== PAGE 11 =====
66-trading-day lookback (~3 months). Selected the 25 lowest-beta stocks to comprise the
MVP (small N). Used Herssein's (2016) procedure to derive allocations from historical
returns only.

Procedure: build a matrix of daily historical returns for each of the 25 assets over the
previous quarter; compute its covariance matrix V; using a ones-vector I and a vector of
each asset's average return R:

mvp_weights = V^-1 R + V^-1 I

(V^-1 = inverse covariance matrix). NO constraints imposed (no max-allocation cap). This
formulation allows BOTH LONG AND SHORT positions -- sign of each weight is
determined by whether that asset's historical average return was positive or negative.

Average exposure: 65% long / 35% short (market tends to rise); closer to 55%/45% during
market declines.

5 Results and Evaluation
Backtest period: 2007-01-01 to 2016-12-31 (10 years, covering both up- and down-trends).
Benchmark: S&P 500 index.

5.1 Momentum Oscillator Performance
Long-short beta-neutral: long the top-ranked quintile (evenly weighted), short the
bottom-ranked quintile (evenly weighted), 50%/50% capital split (dollar-neutral, beta ~0).
Annual return only 2.5%. Beta = -0.01 ("truly a 'pure alpha' strategy" -- returns
independent of market movement). Max drawdown 8.7% (vs S&P 500's >50%). Performance
was largely unaffected by the 2007-2009 crash.

Since markets tend to rise ("perhaps their only genuinely predictable feature," citing the
authors' own prior work), the paper proposes tilting

===== PAGE 12 =====
capital allocation toward the long side to capture this, while staying within a controlled
target beta.

[Figure 3: cumulative performance, unleveraged strategies, 2007-2016 -- ML Dollar
Neutral, ML 55L/45S, Minimum Variance Portfolio, Combined Dollar Neutral, Combined
55L/45S. MVP (grey) is the standout top performer for most of the period, ending highest
(~1.5M) by 2016; the others cluster together lower.]

Assigning 55% long / 45% short: returns boosted by >10 percentage points over the 10-year
period; Sharpe rises from 0.58 to 0.70. Market beta stays low at 0.1 (within the +-0.3
Quantopian bound). Max drawdown actually LOWER at 8.5% (vs dollar-neutral's 8.7%).

5.2 Minimum Variance Portfolio Performance
MVP outperformed the momentum-oscillator strategy in both returns and Sharpe, while
only marginally more volatile: 54.1% return, Sharpe 0.89, beta 0.06 (miniscule benchmark
exposure), max drawdown 8.9%. Since the MVP is dynamically weighted with such a low
beta, authors conclude returns stem from "a combination of both clever stock picking and
intelligent asset weighting."

5.3 Combined Strategies Performance
Combining both strategies in parallel improved performance further: absolute returns
necessarily lower than MVP alone (up to 38.8% at 55%L/45%S) but volatility also lower,
making the combo "more suitable for leverage." Sharpe Ratio = 0.92.

5.4 Leveraged Results
Leveraging = borrowing capital to reinvest, magnifying gains/losses. Authors chose a
leverage factor of 2 (to balance magnified returns against magnified drawdown/vol).

[Figure 4: cumulative performance, leveraged strategies + S&P 500 benchmark,
2007-2016.]

===== PAGE 15 =====
Leverage magnifies returns, beta, max drawdown, and volatility by the leverage factor,
while Sharpe is "relatively unaffected" (authors note a slight Sharpe INCREASE under
leverage, attributed to excess positive returns themselves compounding/being
reinvested faster, offsetting financing costs -- no explicit borrowing-cost rate is given
anywhere in the paper).

Table 2 shows MVP has a LOWER Sharpe than either combined strategy, interpreted as MVP
returns coming from taking on more risk (larger max drawdown, persistently higher
volatility) -- i.e. combining independent strategies "lowers risk while maintaining profits."

5.5 Live Trading and Competition Performance
Quantopian competitions: scored on 6 months of out-of-sample simulated live trading plus
a prior 2-year backtest; ranked metrics averaged across competitors.

Authors entered the combined 55/45 momentum-oscillator/MVP strategy. After 26 weeks:
84th leaderboard position (out of thousands of algorithms globally). Unleveraged returns
since going live: 1.2%. Sharpe Ratio over that 6-month live window: 1.35.

Future work listed before any real capital allocation:
1. Keep the portfolio dollar-neutral at all times (possibly replacing MVP with a long-short
betting-against-beta factor).
2. Keep the portfolio sector-neutral (max 60/40 long-short exposure per sector).
3. Shorten rebalancing to weekly instead of monthly, to constrain neutrality drift (may
increase rebalancing costs).

6 Conclusion
Combined long-short momentum-oscillator + minimum-volatility strategy outperforms
S&P 500 on net returns and risk/profitability metrics. The highest-Sharpe strategy achieves
>9%/yr, "marginally more than the benchmark, but with significantly less risk."

===== PAGE 16 =====
With greater risk tolerance (e.g. leverage 2.5:1), returns of just under 140% (2007-2017)
would outperform the market by ~50%. [Figure 5: Best Strategy Leveraged 2.5:1 vs S&P
500 (SPY), 2007-2016 -- strategy line consistently above the benchmark line for most of
the period, both trending up, strategy climbing to ~2.4M vs benchmark's ~1.9M by the end.]
Max drawdown at 2.5:1 leverage: 19.1% (vs market's >50%).

Under Quantopian's competition rules over the 10-year backtest, the strategy "falls just
short" of Quantopian's own recommended Sharpe >= 1 threshold, managing 0.96. It does
stay within the +-0.3 beta bracket, keeps leverage < 3, and avoids excessively large
drawdowns.

Conclusion (paper's own words): "the success of the algorithm in competitive live trading
provides evidence that smart beta strategies can be effective, and that combining multiple
smart beta strategies can be even more so."
